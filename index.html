The History

The Pair That Was Never Meant to Be Parted

By Amin, the present owner Three generations

My grandfather bought this carpet more than sixty years ago. When he died it passed to the family, and it has been ours ever since. It came to the United States in 2018. It is looking for a new home now, and this is its history as we know it.

There are carpets that are made and carpets that are commissioned. This one was commissioned — twice over, as a pair, on a single cartoon. When the pair came off the loom the cartoon went with it. That is the ordinary practice for work at this level, and it is why there are two of these carpets in the world and there will never be a third.

Nain, and the Weavers Who Made It

Nain sits at the edge of the central Iranian desert, and until the 1930s it wove cloth, not carpets. When the market for its fine woollen cloaks collapsed, its weavers turned the same thread count to the loom. That is the whole reason a Nain looks the way it does: it was made by people already working at a scale finer than carpet weaving normally asks for.

You can see it in the medallion. The lattice does not repeat so much as radiate, each cartouche drawn slightly differently as the rings widen, and the drawing holds together across twenty-one feet without a break in the rhythm. Work of that consistency, at that size, is measured in years on the loom rather than months.

Two were woven, and the cartoon went with them. There will never be a third.

Wool and Silk on Silk

The foundation — warp and weft both — is silk. That is the distinction separating a fine Nain from a very fine one. A silk foundation lets the weaver pack knots more densely than a cotton foundation will bear, and it gives the finished carpet a drape and a weight you feel the moment a corner is lifted. The pile is wool, with silk worked through the outlines so the drawing lifts and changes as you walk around it.

The Second Carpet

The other of the pair stayed in Iran. As our family tells it, it belonged to the Shah, and after the revolution it passed to Rafsanjani; it is understood to lie today in a state reception room of the presidential complex. The Shah's wife, Farah, is said to have spent years looking for its twin — which is to say, for this carpet.

A published photograph of that room, and a frank account of what it does and does not establish, appear further down this page. We would rather set out the claim and its limits than overstate it.

Sixty Years, and Its Condition Today

In 2018 we shipped it to the United States, where it has been ever since. It has been lived with rather than locked away — used, folded, and looked after. For a carpet of sixty years that is the better history: a rug that has been walked on and cared for wears more evenly than one that has spent decades rolled in a corner.

Add any restoration, repiling or edge work here, and when it was last professionally washed. A frank condition note raises the price of a great carpet; it never lowers it. A full written report goes to any serious enquirer.

— Amin, on behalf of the family

The Second Carpet

The Other of the Pair

Only two were woven to this cartoon. This is where the family understands the second one to be.

The family's account is that two carpets were woven to this cartoon and no more, and that the second never left Iran: that it belonged to the Shah, passed after the revolution to Rafsanjani, and lies today in a state reception room of the presidential complex. Farah Pahlavi, the Shah's wife, is said to have spent years looking for its twin.

The carpet in the photograph above is the right family: a cream field carrying a dense lattice of dark-outlined cartouches, drawn at the scale a room like that one demands. It is the kind of carpet that ends up on that floor.

What this photograph does and does not establish

It shows a large ivory-ground Nain of this design family in that room. It is not, by itself, an identification of one specific carpet, and this page does not offer it as one — carpets of this class share a common design vocabulary, and a firm identification would need higher-resolution imagery and a point-by-point comparison of the drawing. Prospective buyers are invited to make that comparison themselves, and the owner will supply everything he holds towards it. The ownership history of the second carpet is stated as related by the family, and is held as family knowledge rather than documentary record.
