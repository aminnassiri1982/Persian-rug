## Headline

> The Pair That Was Never Meant to Be Parted

*Change this if you have something better. The best headline for a carpet like this states
the single most unusual fact about it and nothing else.*

## Opening paragraph (the lede)

*Where you first saw it. Who was in the room. What it was lying on. The light. The details
a photograph cannot carry are the ones that sell a carpet at this level.*

## Nain, and the weavers who made it

Nain sits at the edge of the central Iranian desert, and until the 1930s it wove cloth, not
carpets. When the market for its fine woollen cloaks collapsed, its weavers turned the same
thread count to the loom — which is the whole reason a Nain looks the way it does.

*Then add what you know about this workshop specifically: the master weaver, the knot count,
the years on the loom, the signature in the field if there is one.*

## Wool and silk on silk

*The foundation is silk, warp and weft — say so plainly and say why it matters: it allows a
higher knot count than cotton will bear, and it gives the carpet its weight and drape. The
pile is wool with silk worked through the outlines, so the drawing shifts as you walk
around it.*

## The second carpet

*This is the section that carries the most weight, so write it plainly and exactly.*

- Who owned the pair, and when.
- How and when the two were separated.
- Where the second carpet is understood to be today.
- **Which claims you can document, and which rest on family account.** Keep these apart in
  the writing. A serious buyer will ask, and the distinction raises your credibility rather
  than lowering it. Anything you can support — photographs, correspondence, a bill of sale,
  a customs document, an old appraisal — belongs in the provenance file you release to
  registered bidders.

## Sixty years, and its condition today

*Pile height. Any restoration, repiling or reweaving. The state of fringes and selvedges.
Sun fade. Moth. When it was last professionally washed, and by whom. A frank condition note
raises the price of a great carpet — it never lowers it.*
